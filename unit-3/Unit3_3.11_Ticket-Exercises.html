Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three trouble tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you?
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them yourself.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution. Answer them in your notes.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of output from the network being troubleshot — what you would see on a screen, read on a label, or hold in your hand. They are simplified to show what matters, the way a performance-based question on the N10-009 exam presents a tool’s output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA’s troubleshooting methodology it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and identify potential effects
  5. Implement the solution or escalate as necessary
  6. Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures if applicable
  7. Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned throughout the process

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Ticket 1 of 3The Printer Nobody Can ReachTuesday · Objective 1.7 — subnet masks · About 20 minutes
Ticket NET-3-0302
RequesterCarla Nguyen, Office Lead, Brightway Realty (18 staff)
Site / DeviceMain office · printer PRN-01 and workstations on 192.168.20.0/24
SeverityMedium
CategoryConnectivity — addressing

The Call

“Our new printer only works for two people. Everyone else gets “printer offline.” The installer set it up with a static address and said it was all done.”

Stop Here
Before you look at the exhibits, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you?

Exhibit A — Printer network settings and a workstation’s ipconfig

SettingPrinter PRN-01Workstation WS-07
IPv4 address192.168.20.9192.168.20.40
Subnet mask255.255.255.240255.255.255.0
Default gateway192.168.20.1192.168.20.1

Exhibit B — Ping from the printer’s diagnostic page

ping 192.168.20.5   ... reply
ping 192.168.20.12  ... reply
ping 192.168.20.40  ... request timed out
ping 192.168.20.1   ... reply

Exhibit C — The printer’s subnet, worked

192.168.20.9 /28  -> mask 255.255.255.240, block size 16
network 192.168.20.0   broadcast 192.168.20.15
usable  192.168.20.1 - 192.168.20.14

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Found: the installer gave the printer a /28 mask. To the printer, only .1–.14 are local; every other workstation is “remote,” so its replies go to the gateway, which does not hairpin them back onto the same segment. The two working users are at .5 and .12.

Action: set the printer mask to 255.255.255.0. Verified: test page from WS-07 and three others. Follow-up: documented the printer’s address in the IP plan and reserved it in DHCP.

Look Back

  1. How did the two working users’ addresses point straight at the cause?
  2. Explain to Carla, without the word “mask,” why the printer could hear some people and not others.
Ticket 2 of 3Half the Building Went DarkWednesday · Objective 2.2 — spanning tree · About 20 minutes
Ticket NET-3-0311
RequesterIsaac Brennan, Facilities, Harmon Logistics
Site / DeviceFloors 2–3 · new access switch SW-3B and its two uplinks
SeverityCritical
CategoryOutage — switching

The Call

“Right after the contractor plugged in the second cable for “redundancy,” everything on floors 2 and 3 stopped. The lights on the switches are blinking like crazy.”

Stop Here
Before you look at the exhibits, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you?

Exhibit A — show interfaces on the distribution switch, both uplinks to SW-3B

Gi1/0/23  input rate 912,440 pkts/sec   broadcasts 905,118/sec
Gi1/0/24  input rate 914,002 pkts/sec   broadcasts 906,730/sec
CPU utilization 99%

Exhibit B — show spanning-tree on SW-3B

Spanning tree instance(s) for vlan 1 do not exist.
%SPANTREE: spanning-tree is disabled

Exhibit C — Port states after the fix, for comparison

PortRoleState
Gi1/0/23RootForwarding
Gi1/0/24AlternateBlocking

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Found: a broadcast storm. SW-3B arrived with spanning tree disabled; the second uplink created a loop and broadcasts multiplied until the links and switch CPUs saturated.

Action: pulled the second uplink; enabled RSTP; reconnected and confirmed one port in Blocking. Verified: broadcast rate normal; floors restored. Follow-up: added “STP enabled” to the switch build checklist.

Look Back

  1. What in Exhibit A told you this was a loop and not a single failure?
  2. Why is “blocking” the state that makes redundancy safe?

“My desk phone works fine, but my laptop says no internet when it’s plugged into the phone. It worked in my old office down the hall.”

Exhibit A — Laptop ipconfig

Exhibit B — Switch port configuration for office 2B

 description Office-2B
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 99
 switchport voice vlan 20
 power inline auto

Exhibit C — The site’s VLAN plan

VLANNameSubnetDHCP

20Voice10.50.20.0/24Yes
99Unused-Parking—No

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Found: office 2B’s port was left in VLAN 99, the “parking” VLAN for unused ports, so the laptop’s DHCP request reached no server. The phone uses voice VLAN 20, which was set correctly.

Action: switchport access vlan 10. Verified: laptop received 10.50.10.x; phone unaffected. Follow-up: audited the floor’s ports for other VLAN 99 assignments.

Look Back

  1. Why was “the phone works” such useful evidence?
  2. Parking unused ports in a dead VLAN is good practice. What step in the move process would have caught this?
