Availability and Disaster Recovery

Technical Overview

RTO (recovery time objective) is how long a service may be down; RPO (recovery point objective) is how much data, measured in time, may be lost. MTBF measures reliability; MTTR measures repair speed. Sites: hot (running, current data, minutes), warm (equipment ready, data restored, hours), cold (space and power only, days), and cloud sites. Designs: active-active (all members carry load) and active-passive (a standby takes over). Techniques: load balancing, multipathing, NIC teaming, redundant power supplies, clustering, and FHRP for gateways. Backups: full, incremental (changes since the last backup), differential (changes since the last full), snapshots. Testing: tabletop exercises walk the plan on paper; validation tests fail over for real.

RTO and RPO choose the site

An RTO of minutes needs a hot site or active-active; an RPO of minutes needs replication, not nightly backups. Exam scenarios state the two numbers and ask for the design that meets them at the lowest cost — read the numbers first, then the options.

Physical Installations

Technical Overview

Racks are measured in rack units (1U = 1.75 in); align equipment port-side intake or exhaust with the hot-aisle/cold-aisle layout. UPS bridges short outages and conditions power; generators carry long ones; PDUs distribute power in the rack. Plan capacity in watts and amps at the correct voltage. Environment: temperature, humidity (too low invites static, too high condensation), and fire suppression — clean-agent gas protects equipment where water would destroy it. Lockable racks and controlled access protect the hardware itself.

The Cloud and Modern Networks

Technical Overview

Service models: SaaS (the provider runs everything), PaaS (you bring the application), IaaS (you manage the OS and up). Deployment: public, private, hybrid, community. A VPC is your isolated network in a public cloud, filtered by network security groups (instance level) and network ACLs (subnet level), reaching the internet through an internet gateway and private networks through a NAT gateway. Connectivity: site-to-site VPN over the internet, or a direct connect private circuit. Elasticity grows and shrinks with demand; scalability handles more of it; multitenancy shares infrastructure among customers. NFV runs network functions as software. SDN separates the control plane from the data plane; SD-WAN steers traffic across links by policy; VXLAN carries Layer 2 over Layer 3 for data centers; zero trust verifies every request regardless of location; SASE (Secure Access Service Edge) delivers networking and security as a cloud service, and SSE is its security half; infrastructure as code defines networks in version-controlled files and automates them.

Terminology Note

The book’s heading reads “Secure Access Secure Edge”; CompTIA’s term is Secure Access Service Edge (SASE). Use CompTIA’s on the exam.

Security Concepts

Technical Overview

The CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity, availability. Threats exploit vulnerabilities; risk is likelihood times impact. Encryption protects data in transit and at rest. PKI binds identities to public keys with certificates issued by a certificate authority; self-signed certificates are trusted only locally. AAA: authentication (who you are — MFA combines something you know, have, are, or where you are), authorization (what you may do — least privilege, RBAC), accounting (what you did). Protocols: RADIUS, TACACS+, LDAP, SAML, Kerberos, SSO. Deception: honeypots and honeynets. Compliance: PCI DSS, GDPR, data locality. Segmentation: separate IoT, OT/ICS, guest, and BYOD traffic.

Defenses (objective 4.3)

Device hardening: change default credentials, disable unused ports and services, keep firmware current, use SSH instead of Telnet. ACLs permit or deny by address, port, and protocol, read top-down with an implicit deny. Content filtering, URL filtering. Port security and MAC filtering. 802.1X and NAC admit devices only after authentication and posture checks. A screened subnet (DMZ) holds public-facing servers between two firewalls or interfaces. Physical: cameras, locks, badge readers, access-control vestibules.
