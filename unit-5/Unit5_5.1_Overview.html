Seven objectives and twenty-five videos — the widest unit in the course. It answers three questions a network must survive: what happens when something fails, where does the network end now that half of it lives in the cloud, and who is trying to break it. The first full-length practice exam opens this unit.

Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, you will be able to:

  1. Judge an availability design — load balancing, multipathing, NIC teaming, redundant hardware, active/active versus active/passive — against stated RTO and RPO
  2. Explain disaster recovery sites, backups, and testing, and the physical-installation factors of power, environment, and racks
  3. Summarize cloud service and deployment models and connectivity options, and the modern use cases of SDN, SD-WAN, VXLAN, zero trust, SASE/SSE, and infrastructure as code
  4. Explain the CIA triad, encryption, certificates and PKI, AAA, IAM, physical security, deception, segmentation, and compliance
  5. Classify an attack from its symptoms — DoS and DDoS, VLAN hopping, MAC flooding, ARP and DNS poisoning, rogue devices, spoofing, social engineering, malware
  6. Apply the defense that stops it — device hardening, ACLs, content filtering, segmentation, 802.1X, NAC, port security, MAC filtering, screened subnets
Technical Overview

Unit 5 addresses objectives 3.3 (disaster recovery: RPO, RTO, MTTR, MTBF; cold, warm, and hot sites; active-active and active-passive; tabletop exercises and validation tests), 2.4 (physical installations: IDF/MDF, rack size, port-side exhaust and intake, cabling, lockable racks, power — UPS, PDU, generator, capacity, voltage — and environment — humidity, fire suppression, temperature), 1.3 (cloud: NFV, VPC, network security groups and lists, cloud gateways, VPN and direct connect, deployment and service models, scalability, elasticity, multitenancy), the remainder of 1.8 (SDN, SD-WAN, VXLAN, zero trust, SASE/SSE, infrastructure as code), 4.1 (security concepts), 4.2 (attacks), and 4.3 (defenses).

In Plain Terms

Everything fails eventually — a power supply, a building, a cloud region — and resilience is deciding in advance how long you can be down and how much data you can lose. Security is the same discipline aimed at people instead of accidents. A technician who can say “this outage is within our four-hour RTO” or “those duplicate gateway replies are ARP poisoning” is the one trusted with the important systems.

Why We Start Here

Security comes before troubleshooting because you cannot troubleshoot a rogue DHCP server or a poisoned ARP cache until you can recognize one. Resilience and cloud sit beside security because they are the same question — what happens when something goes wrong — asked about hardware, providers, and attackers. Unit 6 assumes all three.

CompTIA Network+ Passes at 720

The exam is scored on a 100–900 scale and passes at 720. Because the scale starts at 100 rather than zero, 720 works out to about 78% of raw points. That is a high bar: a student who treats 70% on a practice set as “good enough” fails the real exam. This course’s mastery gate of 800 exists so that your exam day has a cushion.
