Instructions — Read Before You Begin

What This Is

Three trouble tickets, one for each class meeting this unit. Work each one on your own. Every ticket is a real kind of call, with real evidence, and three questions that ask what a technician would actually do next. Plan on about 20 minutes per ticket.

How to Work a Ticket

  1. Read the call, then stop. Before you look at any evidence, write one sentence in your notes: what do you think is wrong, and how sure are you?
  2. Study the exhibits as if you had just collected them yourself.
  3. Answer the three questions. Each one stands on its own. You get one attempt per question.
  4. Write your own progress note, then compare it with the Ticket Solution.
  5. Look back. The reflection prompts sit inside the Ticket Solution. Answer them in your notes.

What You Will See When You Answer

  • A flag that says Correct or Incorrect — nothing more. You will not be shown which answer was right.
  • Answer all three correctly and the Ticket Solution opens on its own. Otherwise, click Ticket Solution to open it yourself — after you have written your note.
  • Reset These Questions clears a ticket so you can work it again.

About the Exhibits

Each ticket includes exhibits labeled A, B, and C. Every exhibit is representative of output from the network being troubleshot — what you would see on a screen, read on a label, or hold in your hand. They are simplified to show what matters, the way a performance-based question on the N10-009 exam presents a tool’s output. Read them the way a technician reads a screen: column headings first, then the values that stand out, then the details that do not fit.

The Method Behind Every Ticket

Each question is tagged with the step of CompTIA’s troubleshooting methodology it exercises. You will use this method for the rest of your career; these tickets are where it becomes a habit.

  1. Identify the problem
  2. Establish a theory of probable cause
  3. Test the theory to determine the cause
  4. Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and identify potential effects
  5. Implement the solution or escalate as necessary
  6. Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures if applicable
  7. Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned throughout the process

About the Times and Names

Every person, company, and device in these tickets is fictional. Times shown inside a ticket are part of the scenario, not course deadlines — course due dates are always in the Course Schedule.

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: every Monroe workstation has limited connectivity since the router replacement.

Found: workstations hold APIPA addresses (169.254.x.x), so their DHCP Discover broadcasts are going unanswered. The DHCP server at headquarters is healthy and the Monroe scope is active with zero leases — it never received a request. Discover is a broadcast, and broadcasts do not cross a router unless the router relays them: the old router’s ip helper-address 10.1.0.10 on the VLAN 20 interface was not carried to the new one.

Action: added ip helper-address 10.1.0.10 to GigabitEthernet0/1 on the new router; ran ipconfig /renew on one workstation and received 10.20.0.100 with the correct gateway and DNS servers.

Verified: scope shows leases climbing; three workstations tested to the core banking application; member service resumed by 9:05 AM.

Follow-up: compared the full running configuration against Friday’s backup and found no other omissions; asked the vendor for their migration checklist and added “verify DHCP relay on every user VLAN” to ours.

Look Back

  1. Your first theory probably involved the DHCP server. Exhibit B cleared it in one line. What in Exhibit A should have told you the server was never reached?
  2. Explain to Tomás, in two sentences with no acronyms, why replacing a router can stop computers from getting addresses.
  3. The vendor’s router “worked” — it routed. What test would have caught this on Saturday before anyone left?

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: “internet down” for the whole office; established sessions kept working; sites reachable by IP.

Found: connectivity is fine (ping to 8.8.8.8 succeeds); name resolution fails. The workstations’ DNS server is 192.168.40.250, which does not respond; the same name resolves instantly against 1.1.1.1. The DHCP option 006 (DNS Servers) was changed last night from 192.168.40.10 — the working domain controller — to .250, an address with nothing listening on port 53. As eight-hour leases renewed through the morning, workstations picked up the bad option one by one.

Action: restored option 006 to 192.168.40.10; ran ipconfig /renew and ipconfig /flushdns on the affected workstations.

Verified: nslookup against the renewed address resolves; browsers load by name; confirmed with Leah.

Follow-up: asked the contractor what .250 was meant to be (a planned new DNS server, not yet built); put a change-management note on the DHCP scope so option changes are announced before they are made.

Look Back

  1. Leah said “the internet is down.” Which two facts in her call told you it was not, before you ran a single command?
  2. Why did the problem spread over twenty minutes instead of hitting everyone at once? Name the DHCP mechanism.
  3. Write the one-line instruction you would give the contractor so this does not happen with the next change.
Ticket 3 of 3The Scanner Stopped EmailingThursday · Objectives 1.4, 1.2 — ports, firewall rules · About 20 minutes
Ticket NET-2-0224
RequesterDevon Ashby, Practice Administrator, Lakeshore Pediatrics (two locations)
Site / DeviceFront-office multifunction printer MFP-FRONT, 10.8.1.60, scan-to-email
SeverityMedium — one workflow
CategoryService — mail

The Call

“The copier used to email scans straight to us. Since the new firewall went in last week it just says “send failed.” Printing is fine, and our regular email on the computers works. The firewall company says their box is working perfectly.”

Close the Ticket

Write your own progress note and resolution first. Then open the Ticket Solution below to compare.

Ticket Solution
Resolution

Reported: scan-to-email fails since the firewall replacement; printing and desktop mail unaffected.

Found: the scanner submits mail on TCP 587 (SMTP with STARTTLS). The new outbound rule set allows web, DNS, and IMAP 993 (which is why desktop mail still reads), explicitly denies SMTP 25 from the office subnet, and ends with a deny-all. Port 587 matches no allow rule and falls to rule 5. The desktop mail client sends through the provider’s web service on 443, so users never noticed. A port test from a workstation confirms 587 is blocked and 443 is open.

Action: added an outbound allow rule above the deny-all for 10.8.1.60 only to smtp.mailhost.example on TCP 587; left SMTP 25 denied.

Verified: the port test succeeds; a test scan arrived in the inbox; confirmed with Devon.

Follow-up: documented the scanner’s dependency (587 STARTTLS to the mail host) in the firewall change record so the next rule review keeps it.

Look Back

  1. The firewall vendor said the box was “working perfectly.” It was. What is the difference between a device working and a device configured for your needs?
  2. Why did users’ email keep working while the scanner’s did not? Name the two ports.
  3. The fix allowed 587 for one host only. Why not for the whole subnet?
