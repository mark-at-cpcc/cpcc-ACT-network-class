Three classroom labs, optional and run as time permits, and one take-home lab, expected. None of these are graded; the NETLAB labs in Section 2.8 are.

Lab 2.6.1 — Device Line-Up

Estimated Time: 30 minutes · Classroom · Objective 1.2

The lab’s hardware on the bench — a NIC, a switch, a router, an access point, a firewall appliance, and photographs of what the room does not have. Identify each, name the OSI layer it works at, and say what it reads and what it decides.

You Will Need

  • The bench hardware and the instructor’s photo cards (load balancer, proxy, WLC, NAS)

Steps

  1. Identify each device from its ports and labels before reading any model number.
  2. Place it on the OSI layer strip taped to the bench.
  3. Say the two questions for each one — what it reads, what it decides — to a partner, who checks you against the table in Section 2.3.
  4. Pick the device for three scenarios the instructor reads out (“route between five VLANs in one building,” “spread web traffic across three servers,” “block an application by name”).

What to Record

The devices you placed on the wrong layer. There is usually one, and it is usually the firewall.

2

Lab 2.6.2 — Port Relay

Estimated Time: 30 minutes · Classroom · Objective 1.4

A timed team relay. Twenty-five protocol cards on one table, port-number cards on another; teams run them into pairs, then sort the pairs into TCP, UDP, and secure.

You Will Need

  • The printed card packet (instructor provides)

Steps

  1. Round one, five minutes: match every protocol to its port.
  2. Round two, three minutes: sort the matched pairs by transport — TCP, UDP, or both.
  3. Round three, two minutes: pull out every secure protocol and lay its insecure twin beside it (SSH/Telnet, HTTPS/HTTP, LDAPS/LDAP, SFTP/FTP).
  4. Score against the Section 2.3 table; the losing team explains the ones they missed.

What to Record

Your personal misses. Those go on the index card you started in Section 2.4.

3

Lab 2.6.3 — Watch DHCP Happen

Estimated Time: 45 minutes · Classroom · Objective 3.4

Capture a DHCP lease on a lab PC with Wireshark and read the four messages — Discover, Offer, Request, Acknowledge — and the options inside them.

You Will Need

  • A lab PC with Wireshark installed, on the lab network (the instructor confirms the capture is authorized)

Steps

  1. Start a capture on the wired interface with the display filter dhcp (older versions: bootp).
  2. Release and renew: ipconfig /release then ipconfig /renew.
  3. Find the four packets. Note the source and destination addresses of each — which are broadcast, and why.
  4. Open the Offer and read the options: the subnet mask, the router (option 3), the DNS servers (option 6), the lease time (option 51), and the server identifier.
  5. Compare with ipconfig /all. Every number on the screen came from one of those options.

What to Record

The lease time and the DHCP server’s address. On the unit test, the question “which message is a broadcast?” is answered by what you saw in step 3.

H

Lab 2.6.4 — Take-Home: Where Does Your Name Go?

Estimated Time: 1 hour · At home · Objectives 3.4, 5.5

Use the command line on your own machine to see how a name becomes an address, and to compare the answers two different resolvers give. It continues the map you drew in Unit 1 — now with the services filled in.

Your Own Equipment Only

Run this lab only on the network you own or are explicitly authorized to use. These commands are read-only, but the rule is the same for every lab.

Steps

  1. Find your resolver. ipconfig /all (Windows) or scutil --dns / resolvectl status (macOS, Linux). Record the DNS server address and say where it came from (your DHCP lease, almost certainly).
  2. Resolve three names with nslookup: your college’s site, a large public site, and a name you expect to fail. Record the answer, the record type (A or AAAA), and whether the answer was authoritative.
  3. Ask a different resolver: nslookup <name> 1.1.1.1 and nslookup <name> 8.8.8.8. Do the answers differ? Note the TTL if your tool shows it.
  4. Look up a mail record: nslookup -type=MX <your college’s domain>. Record the MX hosts and their priorities.
  5. Check time: w32tm /query /status (Windows) or sntp / timedatectl (macOS, Linux). Record the time source and the stratum if shown.

What to Record

Write it up as a ticket: device, what you ran, what you found, and the one answer that surprised you. Add the DNS server and time source to the logical diagram from Unit 1.

Ticket of the Day

One ticket per class meeting, worked on your own

This unit’s three tickets live in Section 2.11 Ticket Exercises: Everyone Got 169.254 (Tuesday), The Internet Is Down, Except It Isn’t (Wednesday), and The Scanner Stopped Emailing (Thursday). Each takes about twenty minutes.

What These Labs Give You

Objective 1.4 is recall, and recall is built by retrieval under pressure — which is what the relay is. Objective 3.4 is “given a scenario, implement,” and the only way to recognize a DHCP failure on the exam is to have watched a DHCP success in a capture. The take-home lab turns nslookup into a tool you reach for, which Unit 6 will assume.
