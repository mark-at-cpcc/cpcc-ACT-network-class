Technical Overview

The 2.4 GHz band reaches farther and penetrates walls better but has only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels in the US (1, 6, 11) and more interference (microwaves, Bluetooth). 5 GHz has many more channels and supports wider channel bonding (40, 80, or 160 MHz) at shorter range; some channels require DFS to avoid radar. 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E) adds clean spectrum. Co-channel interference is APs on the same channel competing for airtime; adjacent-channel interference is overlap between nearby channels. Antennas are omnidirectional (all around) or directional (Yagi, patch, parabolic — focused). The SSID is the network name; the BSSID is an AP radio’s MAC; an ESSID is one SSID across many APs for roaming. APs are autonomous (configured one by one) or lightweight under a wireless LAN controller. Modes: infrastructure, ad hoc (IBSS), and mesh.

Wireless Security

WPA3 is current (SAE replaces the pre-shared-key handshake); WPA2 with AES/CCMP is acceptable; WEP and WPA/TKIP are obsolete. Personal (PSK) uses one shared passphrase; Enterprise uses 802.1X with a RADIUS server so each user authenticates individually. Captive portals present a web page before granting access; guest networks isolate visitors from internal resources; geofencing limits access by location.

Plan channels like a checkerboard

In 2.4 GHz, neighboring APs alternate 1, 6, 11 so no two adjacent APs share a channel. More APs on the same channel is not more Wi-Fi — it is more contention. When a scenario says “we added access points and it got slower,” the answer is almost always channel planning or transmit power, not another AP.

Where Students Trip

A stronger signal is not always better: an AP turned up too loud makes distant clients cling to it instead of roaming to a closer one (the “sticky client”). Site-survey questions reward lowering power and adding cells over raising power.

Remote Access and Management

Technical Overview

A site-to-site VPN joins two networks through gateways; a client-to-site (remote-access) VPN joins one user’s device to the network; a clientless VPN runs in the browser over TLS. Full tunnel sends all of the client’s traffic through the VPN; split tunnel sends only corporate traffic through it and the rest directly to the internet. Management methods: SSH (encrypted command line), GUI (web interface over HTTPS), API (programmatic, for automation), and the console port (rollover or USB cable, works with no network at all). A jump box is a hardened host administrators connect through to reach management interfaces. In-band management travels over the production network; out-of-band uses a separate path (a console server, cellular modem, or management network) that still works when production is down.

Monitoring

Technical Overview

SNMP: a manager (NMS) polls agents on UDP 161 and receives traps on UDP 162; data is organized in the MIB and addressed by OIDs; SNMPv1/v2c authenticate with plaintext community strings, SNMPv3 adds authentication and encryption. Flow data (NetFlow, sFlow, IPFIX) records who talked to whom, how much, and on which ports. Packet capture records the packets themselves, usually through a port mirror or tap. A baseline is normal performance measured over time, so you can tell what changed. Syslog (UDP 514) sends device logs to a collector with severity levels 0 (emergency) through 7 (debug); a SIEM aggregates and correlates logs from many sources. Metrics to know: temperature, CPU and memory, bandwidth, latency, jitter, interface errors and discards.

Documentation, Processes, and Policy

Technical Overview

Diagrams: physical (where things are and how they are cabled), logical (subnets, VLANs, routing), rack, cable maps, and Layer 1/2/3 views. IPAM tracks address allocation. Asset inventory and the life cycle (procure, deploy, maintain, end-of-life, end-of-support, decommission and sanitize). Change management requests, reviews, schedules, documents, and can roll back every change; configuration management keeps baseline configurations and backups. SOPs standardize routine tasks. Policies: acceptable use, password, BYOD, remote access, onboarding and offboarding, data loss prevention. Agreements: SLA (service levels), NDA, MOU.

On the Job

A switch outage at 2 AM is resolved in ten minutes when the on-call technician has a current diagram, the last config backup, and a change log showing what was altered yesterday. Without them, it is resolved at 10 AM. Objective 3.1 is the difference.
